Exploring state-level GDP per capita with regression

A 50-state regression study with a coefficient interpreter, documented diagnostics and a clear sensitivity gap.

Explain the equation and its assumptions

I led much of a three-person STAT 3220 study of GDP per capita across U.S. states. The project used a log-response linear regression after exploratory screening and diagnosis; the key technical question is what the fitted coefficients actually mean and what can undermine them.

Reported final modellog(GDP per capita) = 9.434 + 0.00003030 × R&D + 0.02351 × labor force + 0.002664 × urban shareR&D is spending per person in dollars; labor-force participation and urban share are reported as percentage values.

01 / From candidates to the reported model

Transform

Examine the right-skewed GDP distribution and model its logarithm, then compare continuous and categorical candidate predictors.

Screen

Review VIF and candidate terms; the income-tax indicator was dropped after a reported nested-model F-test (p = 0.0868).

Diagnose

Inspect residuals, normality, influence and PRESS. Removing two influential states requires a retain-versus-remove sensitivity check before strong claims.

02 / Portfolio extension: interpret a hypothetical change

Change each input relative to a baseline state. The calculator uses exp(β × change) − 1 to express the fitted association in GDP per capita. It does not supply a causal policy effect, a prediction interval or an estimate outside the observed data range.

+2.7%approximate modeled GDP-per-capita difference

For one percentage-point rise in labor-force participation alone, exp(0.02351) − 1 ≈ 2.38%. The combined calculation is illustrative and assumes every other modeled variable is held constant.

Inference boundary

The model mixes indicators from 2020–2022 and examines cross-sectional associations. Neighboring states may share shocks; the lack of a time index does not prove independent errors. Coefficients should not be presented as effects of increasing R&D or changing policy.
